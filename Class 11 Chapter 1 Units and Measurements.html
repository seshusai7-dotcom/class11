1. Physical Quantities

1.1 Meaning of a physical quantity

A physical quantity is a measurable property of a physical system that can be expressed as a numerical value multiplied by a unit.

Q = n × u

Here, Q is the physical quantity, n is its numerical value and u is its unit. For example, a rod of length 5 m has numerical value 5 and unit metre.

Change of unit: The numerical value changes when the unit changes, but the physical quantity remains the same. For example, 1 m = 100 cm.

1.2 Classification: scalar and vector quantities

Scalar quantities

Described by magnitude only. Examples: distance, speed, mass, time and energy.

Vector quantities

Require magnitude and direction. Examples: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force and momentum.

1.3 Fundamental quantities

Fundamental quantities are independent quantities that are not expressed in terms of other physical quantities. The SI system has seven base quantities.

3. Dimensions and Dimensional Formulae

3.1 Meaning of dimensions

Dimensions indicate the powers of fundamental quantities used to represent a physical quantity. In mechanics, base dimensions are mass M, length L and time T. Electric current A and temperature K are added when required.

[Q] = [Mᵃ Lᵇ Tᶜ]

3.2 Worked derivations

Velocity

v = displacement / time
[v] = L / T = [LT⁻¹]

Acceleration

a = velocity / time
[a] = [LT⁻¹] / [T] = [LT⁻²]

Force

F = ma
[F] = [M][LT⁻²] = [MLT⁻²]

Work

W = F × displacement
[W] = [MLT⁻²][L] = [ML²T⁻²]

Power

P = W / t
[P] = [ML²T⁻²] / [T] = [ML²T⁻³]

Pressure

p = F / A
[p] = [MLT⁻²] / [L²] = [ML⁻¹T⁻²]
Unit versus dimension: metre per second is a unit of velocity; [LT⁻¹] is its dimensional formula. Units can change across systems, but dimensions remain invariant.

4. Important Dimensional Formulae with Derivations

For every physical quantity, begin with its defining equation, substitute the dimensions of the fundamental quantities, simplify the powers, and write the final dimensional formula. In this section, \(M,L,T,I,\Theta\) represent mass, length, time, electric current and thermodynamic temperature respectively. (The symbol \(I\) is used for current and \(\Theta\) for temperature.)

Method: Physical equation → rearrange for the required quantity → substitute dimensions → simplify → box the result. Angles and pure numbers are dimensionless.

Gravitational constant:
\(F=\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}\Rightarrow G=\frac{Fr^2}{m_1m_2}\)

Spring constant:
\(F=kx\Rightarrow [k]=\frac{[F]}{[x]}=\frac{[MLT^{-2}]}{[L]}=\boxed{[MT^{-2}]}\)

\(L=I_{rot}\omega\Rightarrow [L]=[ML^2][T^{-1}]=\boxed{[ML^2T^{-1}]}\)

Surface tension:
\([S]=[F]/[l]=[MLT^{-2}]/[L]=\boxed{[MT^{-2}]}\)

\(P=\sigma A\Theta^4\Rightarrow [\sigma]=\frac{[P]}{[A][\Theta]^4}=\frac{[ML^2T^{-3}]}{[L^2][\Theta^4]}=\boxed{[MT^{-3}\Theta^{-4}]}\)

\(E=hf\Rightarrow [h]=[E]/[f]=[ML^2T^{-2}]/[T^{-1}]=\boxed{[ML^2T^{-1}]}\)

5. Principle of Homogeneity of Dimensions

In any physically meaningful equation, every term added or subtracted must possess identical dimensions. LHS and RHS dimensions must precisely match.

If A = B + C, then [A] = [B] = [C].

Example 1: v = u + at

[v] = [LT⁻¹], [u] = [LT⁻¹], and [at] = [LT⁻²][T] = [LT⁻¹]. All terms are uniform.

Example 2: s = ut + ½at²

[s] = L; [ut] = [LT⁻¹][T] = L; [at²] = [LT⁻²][T²] = L. Dimensionally consistent.

Dimensionless quantities

Examples: strain, relative density, refractive index, coefficient of friction, Poisson's ratio, and plane angle (\(\theta = \frac{\text{arc length}}{\text{radius}} = \frac{L}{L} = 1\)). Trigonometric, exponential, and logarithmic functions must be strictly dimensionless.

6. Applications of Dimensional Analysis

6.1 Checking equations

Testing v² = u² + 2as. Dimensions of v² and u² are [L²T⁻²]. Also [as] = [LT⁻²][L] = [L²T⁻²]. Verified.

6.2 Deriving relations & converting units

Establishes dependencies between variables and converts values across unit systems.

7. Derivations Using Dimensional Analysis

7.1 Simple pendulum time period

Assume T depends on length l and gravitational acceleration g: \(T = k\,l^a g^b\).

[T] = [L]^a[LT^{-2}]^b = [L^{a+b} T^{-2b}]

Comparing powers gives \(a+b=0\) and \(-2b=1\), so \(b=-\frac12\) and \(a=\frac12\). Hence \(T=k\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\).

7.2 Orbital speed of a satellite

Assuming \(v=kG^aM^b r^c\). Comparing powers gives \(a=\frac12\), \(b=\frac12\), and \(c=-\frac12\). Therefore, \(v=k\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}\).

8. Limitations of Dimensional Analysis

  1. Cannot determine pure numerical constants (e.g., \(2,\;\pi,\;\frac12\)).
  2. Fails for additive/subtractive terms in equations.
  3. Cannot handle trigonometric, exponential, or logarithmic functions.
  4. Cannot distinguish between quantities with identical dimensions (e.g., work and torque).
  5. Dimensional correctness does not guarantee physical validity.

9. Unit Conversion

Scaling relation between systems: \(n_2=n_1\left(\frac{M_1}{M_2}\right)^a\left(\frac{L_1}{L_2}\right)^b\left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^c\).

1 N = 10⁵ dyne  |  1 J = 10⁷ erg  |  50 kg/m³ = 0.05 g/cm³

11. Examination Questions with Solutions

Note: Carefully curated Master JEE Academy practice questions modelled after previous years' JEE Main and Advanced patterns.

Question 1: Magnetic field dimensions

Find the dimensional formula of magnetic field B using \(F=qvB\).

[B] = [F]/([q][v]) = [MLT⁻²]/([AT][LT⁻¹]) = [MT⁻²A⁻¹]

Question 2: Maximum fractional error

If \(Q=X^{-2}Y^{3/2}Z^{-2/5}\) and fractional errors in \(X, Y, Z\) are \(0.1, 0.2, 0.5\), find maximum fractional error in \(Q\).

\(\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}=2(0.1)+\frac{3}{2}(0.2)+\frac{2}{5}(0.5)=0.7=70\%\)
